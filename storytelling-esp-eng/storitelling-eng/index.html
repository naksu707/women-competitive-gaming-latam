A data story · Latin America

Women in competitive gaming

They make up almost half of all players. Yet many still join an online match with their mic muted and a username that isn't theirs.

▼ scroll to play ▼
Chapter 1

Who Plays

The classic image of a gamer is a teenage boy in front of a console. The data tells a different story: worldwide, 48 in 100 people who play video games are women. In Brazil, the region's largest market, they are already the majority.

Brazil has one of the longest annual series in the region that breaks down players by gender: the Pesquisa Game Brasil. Its trend line shows that women's presence is not a passing trend. Women have been the majority almost every year, with a single exception.

Chapter 2

The First Controller

A 2024 survey across all 20 Latin American countries asked 220 women gamers when and how they started playing. Almost all of them started as children: 78% before age 12.

But that first controller was rarely handed over by a woman. A brother, a father, a cousin or an uncle: male figures were the gateway for 58%. Women in the family or female friends, for only 15%.

And why do they keep playing? It's not just to pass the time. The most chosen reason is testing their skills and beating challenges, the same drive behind any competitive player.

Chapter 3

The Match

In 2026, the association Mujeres en VG surveyed 2,993 women in 12 Latin American countries. It asked whether they had seen other women being discriminated against, harassed or mistreated in gaming spaces.

0%
said yes. For almost three in four, harassment is part of the landscape.

What does that harassment look like? In Mexico, a 2021 study of 2,000 gamers asked women to describe what they had experienced. The most common form isn't extreme attacks but a constant drip: sexist comments.

Latin America is not an isolated case. When studies from different regions are placed side by side, the Latin American figure is higher than the global ones, and the experience gets worse among younger women.

Chapter 4

Playing in Silence

Faced with harassment, many women don't quit: they adapt. They mute their mic, pick a gender-neutral or male username, or stop playing with strangers.

59%
of women surveyed in Latin America hid their gender when joining matches.
Mujeres en VG, 2024 study, as reported by Tiempo Real (UdeC)
59%
of women in a global study concealed their identity online to avoid harassment.
Lenovo study cited by UNESCO (2024)

Two different studies, one region and the whole world, and the same number: 6 in 10 women players hide.

Hiding also changes who they play with. In Mexico, women play far less with friends than men do, and far more with their partner or children. Their gaming happens in closed, safe circles.

Chapter 5

The Narrowing Ladder

If women are almost half of all players, why are they barely visible in tournaments? Because at every step toward the professional level, their presence shrinks. In Mexico, almost half of amateur players are women, but only 1 in 10 professional players is.

The ladder doesn't narrow for lack of interest. It narrows because the competitive environment, the most exposed one (voice chat, matches with strangers, streaming), is exactly where the harassment we saw in chapter 3 is concentrated.

Chapter 6

Community

Even so, they stay. For 59% of Latin American women gamers, playing is a way to de-stress and forget their worries. And 32% of those surveyed by Mujeres en VG in 2026 work or are looking for work in the industry.

59%
use video games to de-stress in their daily lives.
Treviño González and Cobos Cobos (2025), table 8
32%
work or are looking for work in the video game industry.
Mujeres en VG, 2026 study

To change the lobby from the inside, women players organized. Across the region, collectives have emerged to create safe spaces, their own tournaments and professional networks.

Closing

What the Data Says

  1. They are not a minority. Women are 48% of players worldwide and the majority in Brazil.
  2. Harassment is the norm, not the exception. 73% of Latin American women surveyed in 2026 have witnessed it, and in the 2024 study almost 8 in 10 said they had experienced it.
  3. Adapting comes at a cost. 6 in 10 hide their identity, and women's presence fades away at the professional level.

The problem was never that women don't play. It's that they still have to hide in order to do it.

Methodological note. These figures come from surveys with different samples and questions. Most are not representative of the whole population: the Mujeres en VG and Treviño and Cobos surveys recruit women connected to gaming on a voluntary basis. That's why comparisons across studies show orders of magnitude, not exact differences. In Treviño and Cobos (2025), women were 220 of 1,305 valid responses.
